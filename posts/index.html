What I've learned from failed AI projects

Do you think an AI project is a one-person job? One developer, one “AI person”: build it, ship it, done?

It rarely works that way. I keep seeing the same pattern: it comes down to communication and collaboration inside the team.

A few things that make all the difference:

  1. Involve the people who actually know the process in the room where the AI tool gets built. Unfortunately, that's not usually the case, even though domain knowledge is key.
  2. Understand what stakeholders actually want, not just what was said in the first kickoff meeting. Otherwise, you end up solving the wrong thing really well.
  3. Define the target at the start. What does “good enough” look like? Without a clear metric, a project can run forever and still go nowhere.
  4. Keep the feedback loop open during the whole project. Edge cases tend to magically show up once real users try the tool.

The same lesson, every time: get the right people talking early, and often! 🙂

Where NOT to use AI?

In the rush to adopt AI, a quick reminder: some areas are high-risk under the EU AI Act. HR, education, credit and insurance, biometrics, critical infrastructure, justice, law enforcement, migration and border control.

High-risk = strict rules.

Take HR. AI can help write a job ad or prepare interview questions. No problem. But screening CVs or ranking candidates? AI can't make the decision alone. Under GDPR, a person must make the final decision. And the AI Act will add more: trained human oversight, informing candidates, monitoring the tool. Analyzing a candidate's emotions in a video interview is simply banned.

If you are not sure where your use case fits, the EU has a free questionnaire: the AI Act Compliance Checker.

For me, the question is no longer “is AI capable of doing this?” but “who should decide this?”

A client asked this week: “If we pick the best AI model, it won't make things up anymore, right?” Honestly, no. Even the newest, most powerful models still “hallucinate.” Less often, but it happens.

For example, a recent top model like GPT-5.4 gets it wrong around 7% of the time when summarizing a document*. And it's tricky, since a good model sounds confident even when it's wrong. So we may not even notice!

AI is great, but not perfect. So for any serious decision, someone needs to check. That doesn't mean checking everything, of course. A first draft of an email isn't a financial decision. The higher the stakes, the more control we need.

Paying for the biggest model isn't the only fix either. Asking the AI for its sources or adding simple guardrails often does more 🙂

16 September 2026

€35 million. Or 7% of global turnover.

That's the maximum fine under the AI Act, the EU's regulation on artificial intelligence.

And since August 2, 2026, it's no longer theoretical: authorities now have their full enforcement powers. The legal framework itself has existed since August 2025.

The full timeline:

  • August 2024: the AI Act enters into force, no obligations apply yet
  • February 2025: prohibited practices banned (manipulation, social scoring, etc.)
  • August 2025: obligations for general purpose AI (GPAI) models, governance, and the penalties framework
  • August 2026: full application, including high-risk AI, and full enforcement powers for authorities

3 tiers of penalties to know:

  • Prohibited practices: up to €35M or 7% of global turnover
  • High-risk AI systems: up to €15M or 3% of global turnover
  • Misleading information to authorities: up to €7.5M or 1% of global turnover

Good news for SMEs and startups: it's the lower of the two amounts that applies, unlike large corporations.

Bad news: “I didn't know” has never protected anyone from a regulator.

Not every AI task needs a billion parameters.

I was recently discussing LLM speed and costs with a client who was asking about one of the latest OpenAI models.

“Shouldn't we be using this for everything?” he asked.

My answer: No. In fact, for many workflows, you might need the opposite.

It is becoming increasingly obvious that we don't need GPT-5 just to extract a name from a document. We are seeing a massive shift from “bigger is better” to “efficient is better”.

Small Language Models (SLMs)

Unlike LLMs, SLMs operate with significantly fewer parameters (often in the millions). They trade “world knowledge” for hyper-efficiency in specific tasks. Not to forget that:

  1. Cost & speed: SLMs offer similar performance for specific tasks at a fraction of the inference cost and latency.
  2. Privacy & control: Because they are lightweight, SLMs can run on-premise or locally on edge devices. Your data doesn't need to leave your infrastructure.

The future: a separation of duties

We are heading toward a hybrid architecture.

  • SLMs will handle the high-volume work like preprocessing, classification, PII redaction, guardrails, over-specific tasks.
  • LLMs will act as the “managers,” stepping in only when low confidence is detected or complex reasoning and refinement is needed.

Even Menlo Ventures predicts that by 2026, models will move to the edge, driven by privacy and low latency, with inference costs for non-frontier models approaching $0.

Agent in Production Reality Check: Reliability > Autonomy

A new study of 306 practitioners across 26 domains confirms what many of us have experienced: reliability beats autonomy every single time.

  1. Structure over freestyling. The most successful agents aren't self-determining their paths. 80% of them use structured, static workflows. In production, success is found in predictable subtasks.
  2. Reliability is the #1 challenge. Teams implement massive guardrails, human-written prompts and strict manual control loops over automated, stochastic methods.
  3. Humans are the benchmark. Automated evaluation isn't there yet. 74% of teams rely on human-in-the-loop evaluation, because generic benchmarks can't handle domain-specific nuances.
  4. Quality over latency. 66% of organizations trade speed for correctness, accepting response times of minutes to ensure the output outperforms human baselines.

Production-grade agents survive by operating within tight bounds. To make it to production, we often sacrifice open-ended autonomy for the win of predictability.

From Chaos to Control: A Two-Phase Playbook for Bulletproof LLM Evaluations

Ever pushed a "harmless" prompt change that silently broke your LLM app? Traditional tests fall short for non-deterministic AI, so here is a continuous two-phase strategy to stay in control:

  1. Pre-launch safety net. Before shipping, run a curated, high-quality golden dataset through your CI/CD pipeline. This validates the quality of LLM outputs after any change in prompt, settings, or model version. It may combine deterministic checks (like verifying a keyword's presence) and LLM-as-a-Judge evaluations to catch regressions before they ever see a user.
  2. Watching in the wild. Once in production, capture user traces and monitor performance through automated evaluations on real-world, recent data, running either continuously or on sampled batches (daily/weekly). Techniques like LLM-as-a-Judge enforce quality and safety at scale, complemented by others like semantic similarity, depending on the product's needs.

However, the real magic is the feedback loop. What we learn from Phase 2's production data (edge cases, user behaviors, or failure modes) is used to enrich Phase 1's golden dataset. This constant iteration elevates evaluation from a mere task to the engine that drives continuous improvement.

Why the Future of AI Agents is Small (and Smart)

While LLMs get the spotlight, new NVIDIA research argues the future of AI agents is the Small Language Model (SLM). The reason is simple: most agentic tasks are repetitive, narrow, and structured. They don't always require the heavy compute and cost of LLMs.

Why SLMs are useful for agentic AI:

  • Powerful enough. Modern SLMs (e.g., Hugging Face SmolLM2) match larger models on agent tasks like reasoning and tool calling.
  • More economical and faster. With lower latency and 10-30x cheaper to run, they enable real-time responses with less cloud expenses. Fine-tuning is also dramatically faster.
  • Operationally superior. SLMs are perfect for edge and on-device AI, with better privacy and user control. They ensure easily formatted outputs for reliable tool use.

Suggested approach. The path forward isn't about replacing LLMs, but building smarter:

  • Build hybrid, heterogeneous architectures: modular agents with a mix of models.
  • Fine-tune SLMs when possible for specific, high-volume skills.
  • Gradually migrate sub-agents and repetitive tasks from LLMs to SLMs.

The future is hybrid. This approach is more than a technical fix. It's a move toward responsible and sustainable AI that unlocks cost savings, broader accessibility, and more scalable systems.

18 June 2025

LLMs Get Lost in Conversation, and We're Not Evaluating That Enough

A new large-scale study by Microsoft and Salesforce researchers reveals a critical issue some of us have experienced: LLMs perform on average 39% worse in multi-turn conversations compared to single-turn prompts.

What's going wrong?

  • LLMs jump to premature assumptions and cling to flawed prior outputs.
  • They rarely recover once off track.
  • Lowering temperature, even to zero, doesn't fix it.
  • Restarting conversations often works better than continuing them.
  • Consolidating instructions into one prompt helps, but that's unrealistic for most users.

Benchmarks still focus on fully-specified, single-turn tasks. Multi-turn evaluations are rare, often non-reproducible, and don't reflect real-world use. Let's shift the focus toward multi-turn robustness and reproducibility in model development and evaluation.
